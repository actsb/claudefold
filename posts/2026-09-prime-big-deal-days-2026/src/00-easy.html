What is Prime Big Deal Days, and do you need Prime?

Amazon's second Prime Day, October 6–7, 2026, for Prime members only; a 30-day free trial covers it, and Walmart and Target usually run matching sales the same week.

It's Amazon's second Prime Day: 48 hours in early October, open only to Prime members, built to start holiday shopping early. The deals come in three flavours. Today's Big Deals are the headline discounts that last both days or until stock runs out. Lightning Deals are short and limited — a few hours, a few thousand units, a progress bar to make you panic. Invite-only deals cover the handful of items Amazon expects to sell out (think AirPods and TVs); you request an invitation in advance and get an email if you're picked. Walmart and Target usually run their own sales the same week, which matters: it means the "Prime-exclusive" price is often available to everyone at the store across the street.

In real life: deal day

Five traps that catch smart people

  1. The list-price game. "Was $1,600" means nothing if it has been $900 since March. Amazon's own "Lowest price in 30 days" label is more honest than the strike-through; better still is a price-history check (next section).
  2. The coupon that isn't. A $600 "coupon" on a $1,599 robot is the everyday price wearing a costume. Do the arithmetic on the final number, then compare it to the table above.
  3. Lightning-deal panic. The progress bar ("87% claimed") is designed to stop you thinking. If the item isn't on your list at your price, the bar is irrelevant. It will be back.
  4. Bundles and "renewed" look-alikes. A robot bundled with two years of dust bags, or a "Renewed" AirPods listing next to the new one at $40 less — read the title twice. Renewed is fine if you chose it; it's not fine as a surprise.
  5. Buying the brand-new thing. The AirPods 5 shipped on September 18; whatever Meta announces on the 23rd will be new. Brand-new Apple and Meta hardware is rarely discounted before Black Friday, and often not then. The previous model is the deal.

How to check a price in 20 seconds

  1. Paste the Amazon link into camelcamelcamel.com (free, no account). The chart shows every price the item has had. If today's "deal" is a flat line at the same level, it isn't one.
  2. Or install Keepa's browser extension, which draws the same chart directly on the Amazon page. Five minutes to set up, saves you for years.
  3. Look for "Lowest price in 30 days" under the price on Amazon's own page. Amazon adds it automatically; when it's missing on a "deal," ask why.
  4. Open the brand's store and Walmart in two tabs. EcoFlow, Anker, Jackery, Roborock and Dreame run parallel sales that sometimes beat Amazon by a coupon, and Walmart's October sale routinely matches Prime prices with no membership needed.
  5. Then compare to our table. At or below the number: buy. Above it: the item goes back on the list for Black Friday, seven weeks later.

Quick answers

Do I really need Prime?

For the Prime-exclusive prices, yes. A 30-day free trial covers the event; if you don't want to keep it, cancel before the trial ends and you pay nothing. Many of the same prices also appear at Walmart and Target the same week without any membership.

When exactly is it?

Tuesday and Wednesday, October 6–7, 2026, confirmed by Amazon in mid-September: deals open at 12:01 a.m. PT on October 6 and run 48 hours, one day earlier than 2025's October 7–8.

Are October prices as low as Black Friday?

For robot vacuums, power stations at the 1 kWh size, AirPods and Amazon's best sellers, usually yes — October and November have produced the same lows. For the Sony XM6, the Jackery 2000 v2 and the Bissell Little Green, Black Friday has gone lower. The table's last column tells you which is which.

Are the deals real or is it all list-price theatre?

Both. Real record lows do appear — the AirTag four-pack at $79.99 and the AirPods 4 at $89 were genuine. But thousands of "deals" are the everyday price with a strike-through. The only defence is knowing the real price before you look, which is what the table is for.

What is an invite-only deal?

For a few items Amazon expects to sell out, you request an invitation before the event; if selected, you get an email with a window to buy at the deal price. Request early — it costs nothing — and treat it like any other price: only worth it at or below your number.

Do prices drop further on the second day?

Rarely. Headline deals run both days at one price; what changes is stock. Lightning deals rotate, so an item that sold out on day one can reappear. Waiting for day two mostly just risks the colour or size you wanted.

Can I return something bought in the sale?

Amazon's standard 30-day return window applies to most items bought in October; the extended holiday return period usually covers purchases from November 1. Open the box, test it in the first week, and return it if it isn't right — a robot vacuum that can't handle your rug is not a deal.

Should I buy a power station now or wait for the event?

Wait, unless a storm is in the forecast or you depend on a medical device — then buy now, because the 1 and 2 kWh units sell out within a day once a forecast cone touches a coast. If the C1000 Gen 2 is already under $500 today, that's the October price; take it.

Is it worth buying the Ray-Ban Meta before Connect?

No. Two weeks of waiting is likely to save you $80 on the Gen 2 or get you a new model; the only reason to buy this week is a trip you need them for.

What should I skip entirely?

Anything "AI glasses" at $20–$40 (they're camera glasses with a chatbot app), any AirPods listing without a seller name you recognise, robot vacuums from brands with no US phone support, and anything not on your list. Deal events are for buying what you already wanted at a price you already knew.
