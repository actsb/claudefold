What do unhappy NEO 185 owners say?

Three things come up: the first splash is cold, a hose connection can drip if a washer isn't seated, and the plastic doesn't feel like a $500 seat. Most of it can be fixed in minutes.

What fixed it

  • Drips: check that the rubber washer sits flat inside the T-adapter and that the threads aren't crossed. LUXE's guidance is hand-tight plus about a quarter turn, but one of its documents says otherwise, so follow the manual in your box. One NEO 120 owner stopped a small leak with plumber's tape on the existing fitting, and LUXE sends replacement T-adapters.
  • A nozzle that touches the tank or sticks: LUXE's troubleshooting guide says to move the unit forward; hard-water deposits can also make a nozzle stick.
  • A shifting seat: tighten the seat bolts firmly while holding the nuts underneath. A flat-bottomed seat without outer hinge tabs sits best, per LUXE's manual and a plumber answering on JustAnswer.
  • Pressure that's too strong or too weak: toiletbidet.com, which calls the NEO 185 a good beginner choice, notes that finding the right pressure takes practice. If even the lowest setting is too much, close the toilet's supply valve part of the way, as LUXE suggests.
  • A real failure: it is covered for 18 months, or two years if you registered it, and Amazon's return window covers the first 30 days.

One more thing you may find if you search. ClassAction.org, a site that connects consumers with law firms, lists an investigation into leaking supply lines on LUXE's NEO and NEO Plus models, with some reports of flooding. An investigation is not a finding, and it covers the whole line, not the NEO 185 alone. It does tell you where to look. After installing any bidet, check the hose connections that evening, the next morning and again a week later, and make sure everyone in the house knows where the toilet's shut-off valve is.

Which kind of bidet fits your bathroom?

For most homes the answer is a cold-water attachment: it does the washing, costs less than an electrician's visit to add an outlet, and comes off as easily as it went on if you move.

There are five ways to add a bidet to an American bathroom, and they differ less in how well they clean than in what they need from your house. An attachment needs only the cold line that already feeds your toilet tank. A warm-water attachment borrows hot water from the sink, so it needs a hose run across the floor. A manual bidet seat replaces your seat but still needs no electricity. An electric seat heats the water and the seat and adds a dryer, but it needs a grounded outlet within reach of a short cord. Handheld sprayers and travel bottles are the cheapest of all and ask the most of your aim.

Is the KOHLER PureWash dual-nozzle bidet a better buy?

For most homes, no. The M100 does the same job with the KOHLER name on it, but it usually costs more, it has a small fraction of the NEO 185's owner reviews at the stores we checked, and the leaks its owners describe come from the handle valve rather than from a washer you can re-seat.

PureWash is KOHLER's bidet family. The M models are manual and need no electricity; the E models are electric. The “PureWash Dual-Nozzle Bidet” in many listings is the M100 (model K-22860). It is an under-seat attachment with two self-cleaning wands, one for the rear and one for the front, and one side handle that sets both the spray and the pressure. Two other names in search results are not sold in the US. The M120 is a dual-wand attachment KOHLER launched in India in 2025; it won an iF Design Award in 2026. The M350 is a round-front manual seat, also for India. In the US, the PureWash choices are the M100 attachment, the M300 elongated manual seat (K-98804), the older M250 manual seat, and the electric E-series, which runs from about $400 to about $1,300.

How much does it cost to install, and can you do it yourself?

A non-electric attachment costs nothing to install if you can change a toilet seat: one wrench, about 15 minutes, and the only real risk is a drip from a fitting tightened too hard.

Wirecutter's Doug Mahoney put it simply on The Wirecutter Show in August 2025: if you can install a toilet seat, you can install a cold-water bidet. Vendor guides time it at about 15 minutes with one wrench, TUSHY claims 8.5 minutes, and Good Housekeeping's testers said the TUSHY Classic 3.0 lived up to its "10 minutes or less" promise. The steps are the same for every brand.

  1. Turn the water off and flush. The valve behind the toilet closes clockwise; flushing empties the tank so the fill valve does not spill.
  2. Check the fit before you open the box. Fitment guides from Alpha and Bio Bidet give the numbers: seat bolts about 5.5 inches apart (spacing under 5.5 or over 7.5 inches will not fit), at least 1.5 inches between the bolt holes and the tank, and on one-piece toilets a tank that meets the bowl at roughly a right angle. Skirted toilets usually work but can need extra parts.
  3. Swap the seat onto the attachment. Unbolt the seat, lay the bidet plate over the bolt holes and refit the seat on top. Tighten firmly but not hard: the fittings and the plate are plastic.
  4. Fit the T-adapter. It screws onto the tank's fill valve; your old supply hose goes onto the T and the bidet hose onto its branch. LUXE ships a metal T-adapter with the NEO 120; a brass or metal T is less likely to crack than a plastic one.
  5. Turn the water on slowly and watch. Open the valve a little at a time and look at every joint for a full minute. A drip almost always means a washer that is not seated flat; tightening harder is how plastic cracks.
  6. First wash at the lowest setting. Sit down first, then turn the knob slowly. Let the nozzle rinse itself before and after.

Warm-water attachments such as the NEO 320 and TUSHY Spa add a second hose from the hot-water valve under the bathroom sink, using a 3/8-inch T-adapter. The extra hose is usually 9–10 feet long, and most installs need one small hole through the side of the vanity; renters should check the lease first. Allow 30–45 minutes.

Electric seats need a grounded outlet within reach of a cord that is often only about 4 feet long, and bathroom outlets must be GFCI-protected under the National Electrical Code (NEC 210.8(A)). Manufacturers prohibit extension cords and power strips. If there is no outlet near the toilet, cost guides put an electrician-installed GFCI outlet at $130–$300 (Angi, 2026; about $210 on average) and a new circuit from the panel at $300–$700. That is why a $299 seat can become a $500 project.

Two checks that prevent most returns: measure your bolt spacing and the room behind the bolts before you order, and look for certification to ASME A112.4.2/CSA B45.16, the North American standard for bidet attachments and seats, which covers backflow protection.

Does a bidet really save money?

An attachment pays for itself in well under a year on toilet paper alone; an electric seat is a comfort purchase that takes about five years to pay back, or about eleven if you need a new outlet.

Here is our arithmetic, with every input shown so you can swap in your own. A OnePoll survey of 2,000 Americans, reported by StudyFinds in 2020, put yearly toilet-paper spending at about $182. Brand blogs claim bidet owners cut paper use by 75–80%; we assumed a more cautious 50%, so the paper saved is about $91 a year. A wash uses about an eighth of a gallon according to one vendor's figure, which comes to roughly $8 a year in water and sewer charges for two people at 2026 rates. Electric seats add 140–260 kWh a year at about 18 cents per kWh (EIA's 2026 estimate).

On those numbers the NEO 185 pays for itself in about seven months, and in under five if your household cuts paper by three quarters. A warm-water NEO 320 takes about ten months. A $299 electric seat with an outlet already in place takes about five years, and a $299 seat plus a $210 outlet takes about eleven. None of this puts a value on a warm seat in January or a dryer, which is exactly what the electric-seat money buys. If you are buying a bidet to save money, buy an attachment; if you are buying comfort, buy a seat with your eyes open about the payback.

What about the trees and the power bill?

Toilet paper's environmental cost is real, but some of the numbers that circulate are shaky, so here is what holds up. A 2009 Scientific American column quoted an estimate by Justin Thomas of MetaEfficient: Americans use 36.5 billion rolls a year, the pulping of some 15 million trees, plus about 473 billion gallons of water and 253,000 tons of chlorine for bleaching. The often-repeated claim that one roll takes 37 gallons of water is weaker. Lloyd Alter, who says he started that figure in a 2009 TreeHugger post, now estimates 6 to 13 gallons per roll from manufacturers' environmental product declarations. A bidet wash uses about an eighth of a gallon, a figure from Biolife Technologies quoted in the same Scientific American column. For the paper you still use, NRDC's “Issue with Tissue” scorecards rate brands, and NRDC says paper with recycled content has about a third of the carbon footprint of paper made from forest fibre.

Electric seats add a power bill. TOTO's European site estimates 140–180 kWh a year with energy-saving mode for a four-person household, and 180–260 kWh without it. At about 18 cents per kWh that is roughly $25 to $47 a year. Non-electric attachments, including the warm-water ones that borrow from your water heater, add nothing to the electric bill beyond a little hot water.

What doctors and studies say

  • Cleveland Clinic. Gastroenterologist Christine Lee, MD, calls bidets sanitary and safe: water removes residue better than dry paper, it is gentler on skin, and it can be more comfortable for people with hemorrhoids or fissures. Her caution is to spray front to back at a sensible angle and pressure to avoid vaginal or urinary infections.
  • Mayo Clinic Press. In a profile of gastroenterologist Sarah Umar, MD, bidets come up as a way to ease hemorrhoid symptoms after a bowel movement and cut down on wiping.
  • A review of the evidence. A 2022 systematic review by Baig and colleagues found that habitual use made no difference to the odds of hemorrhoids but may raise the odds of anal itching, and noted two case series of perianal burns or anterior anal fissures.
  • A large survey. In a 2018 study of 10,305 people in Japan, habitual use was not significantly linked to hemorrhoids or urogenital infections, though male habitual users more often reported newly irritated skin around the anus.
  • Pressure matters. A 2011 study in the Journal of Korean Medical Science found that warm water at low or medium pressure with a wide stream lowered anal resting pressure, much like a warm sitz bath.
  • After surgery. A randomized trial in BMC Surgery compared a gentle 38 °C bidet with warm sitz baths after hemorrhoid surgery: pain on day seven was similar, though the trial could not formally prove the bidet was as good.
  • For caregivers. A feasibility study in the American Journal of Occupational Therapy found that an automated bidet reduced the physical help people needed with toilet hygiene.
  • The cautions. A 2021 paper by Tsunoda warns that excessive use may cause anal itching and incontinence, and that hospital patients with weakened immunity should be careful with shared bidet toilets. A 2010 study of 268 women who already had increased discharge found that habitual users more often lacked normal vaginal lactobacilli; it was observational. A 2013 study of 2,545 births in Tokyo found no link between normal bidet use and preterm birth.

Put together, the advice is simple: moderate pressure, a comfortable temperature, front to back, a few seconds rather than a long soak, then pat dry. If you have a medical condition, ask your doctor what suits you. This section is general information, not medical advice.

Quick answers

What is the best bidet for the money in 2026?

For most homes, the LUXE NEO 185: about $50 at LUXE's store, two nozzles, self-cleaning, no outlet needed, and 4.9 out of 5 across about 8,200 Walmart reviews. If you only need a rear wash, the NEO 120 costs about $10 less.

Is the KOHLER PureWash M100 worth it?

It is a capable dual-wand attachment, but it usually costs more than the NEO 185, has far fewer owner reviews (about 50 at Home Depot, averaging 4.0 stars), and some owners report leaks at the handle. Buy it near $60 from a store with easy returns if you want the KOHLER name.

What is the difference between the KOHLER PureWash M100, M120, M300 and M350?

The M100 is a dual-wand attachment sold in the US, and the M120 is a dual-wand attachment sold in India. The M300 is an elongated manual bidet seat sold in the US, and the M350 is a round-front manual seat sold in India. None of them needs electricity.

Will a bidet attachment fit my toilet?

Most two-piece toilets work. Fitment guides look for seat bolts about 5.5 inches apart (under 5.5 or over 7.5 inches won't fit), at least 1.5 inches of room behind the bolts, and on one-piece toilets a tank that meets the bowl at roughly a right angle.

Can I install a bidet myself?

Yes, if you can change a toilet seat. Plan on about 15 minutes with one wrench for a cold-water attachment, and 30 to 45 minutes for a warm-water one that connects to the sink.

Is the water cold?

It is as cool as the water in your supply line. Owners call the first splash a shock and say it feels merely cool after a few weeks. If that is a deal-breaker, the NEO 320 mixes in hot water from the sink without needing an outlet.

Do I still need toilet paper?

A little. Most owners pat dry with a few squares or a small towel, and say they buy far less paper than before.

Is a bidet hygienic?

Cleveland Clinic says washing with water is more sanitary than dry paper and gentler on skin. Choose a self-cleaning nozzle, spray front to back, and clean the nozzle as the manual says.

Are bidets good for hemorrhoids?

Cleveland Clinic and Mayo Clinic Press both describe bidets as a way to ease symptoms by cutting down on wiping. Keep the pressure gentle: a 2022 evidence review linked habitual use with more anal itching. Ask your doctor about your own situation.

Does a bidet raise the water bill?

Barely. At about an eighth of a gallon per wash, two people washing six times a day use roughly 550 gallons a year, about $8 at average 2026 water and sewer rates (our arithmetic).

Do electric bidet seats need a special outlet?

They need a grounded outlet within reach of a short cord, and bathroom outlets must be GFCI-protected under the National Electrical Code. Adding one typically costs $130 to $300 (Angi, 2026). Don't use an extension cord.

Can renters install a bidet?

Usually. A cold-water attachment needs no permanent changes and comes off when you move. Warm-water models may need a small hole in the vanity, so check your lease first.

Should I wait for Prime Big Deal Days?

They run October 6 and 7, 2026. LUXE attachments have dropped in past sales, but no bidet deals had been announced when we checked, and the everyday price is already low.

The bottom line: start with the NEO 185

If you have read this far, you know the NEO 185 isn't perfect. The water is cool, the plastic is plastic, and you should check the connections the morning after you install it. It is still the bidet we would hand a friend first. It costs about as much as a few months of toilet paper and goes on in about 15 minutes. It has the front nozzle the cheaper NEO 120 lacks. And owners at Walmart, Best Buy and Home Depot keep giving the same reasons to recommend it.

Live with it for a month and you will know whether warm water or a heated seat is worth paying for. If it turns out bidets aren't for you, you are out about $50, not $500.
