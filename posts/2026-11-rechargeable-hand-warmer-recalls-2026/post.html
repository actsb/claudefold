Is my rechargeable hand warmer recalled? How do I check?

Turn it over and read the model number printed on the underside, next to a three- or four-digit batch number, then compare it with the eight numbers below. The brand name on top does not settle it.

All three 2026 recall notices put the model number in the same place: the bottom of the warmer (CPSC notices; OCOOPA’s recall page). OCOOPA’s own recall page translates two of the numbers into the names on the box: UT3053 is the “UT3 Lite” and UT3056 is the “UT4 Young”. Kobayashi’s recalled HotHands unit is the electric one only, white with orange and a white HotHands logo; the air-activated packets are not part of it, its recall page says.

Why the brand on top is not enough

Two facts from the notices make the model number the only reliable check. First, Gobi Heat’s recalled model is UT3053, the same number as OCOOPA’s UT3 Lite; neither notice says who built them. Second, Kobayashi’s recall page says its electric HotHands warmer shares its lithium-ion battery and electrical components with the OCOOPA units. So three brands, one design family. The magnetic two-piece pair with a USB-C port and a small light is a shape sold under many names; a seller we have never heard of can carry the same insides. We found a Newegg page whose address says “NewAir” for a UT3 Lite, but it showed OCOOPA branding, so we could not confirm a NewAir version exists.

What do I do if the number matches?

  • Stop using it and do not charge it again. The hazard in the OCOOPA notice is a lithium-ion battery that “can overheat and ignite”.
  • Mark it. Write “RECALLED” on the unit in permanent marker (OCOOPA and HotHands both ask for this; HotHands also wants your claim number on it).
  • Photograph the marking, the model number and the batch number, and submit it on the maker’s recall page: OCOOPA, HotHands or Gobi Heat. OCOOPA lists only an email address (ocooparecalls@ocoopa.cc), no phone.
  • Dispose of it at a household hazardous waste site, after calling ahead. The OCOOPA notice says not to put it in the trash, curbside recycling or a store’s battery drop box.
  • Do not fly with it. The FAA says damaged or recalled batteries must not be carried aboard an aircraft (FAA PackSafe).

Expect the OCOOPA refund to take a while. On Trustpilot, OCOOPA showed 1.9 out of 5 from 37 reviews on November 25, 2026, and recent reviewers describe a claim form whose submit button did nothing (August 8, 2026), “approved” refunds still unpaid six weeks to three months later (September to November 2026), and one request for bank details by email (November 10, 2026). Submit only on ocoopa.com, never through a link in an email you did not expect, and if the firm stops answering, report it at SaferProducts.gov or call CPSC at 800-638-2772.

Recall status, capacity and price we could verify from official pages; runtime only partly; UL certification for none of the picks.

The questions people ask before buying

  • Can I sleep with one in my pocket? No. The Zippo manual and HotHands’ packaging both say not to use them while sleeping.
  • Can I take it on a plane? In the cabin, yes: the TSA allows hand warmers, and the FAA caps lithium-ion batteries at 100 watt-hours with spares in carry-on only. The Zippo’s 5,200 mAh is about 19 Wh at a typical 3.7 volts (our arithmetic; Zippo does not publish the voltage). A recalled one may not fly at all.
  • Is it safe for kids? Packaging and manuals say not for infants or small children, and adult supervision for the rest.

How hot is too hot against your skin?

Hot enough to burn slowly: skin can burn at about 111°F over a few hours and at 122°F in a few minutes, and most warmers reach those numbers.

Japan’s product-safety agency NITE gives the thresholds for these “low-temperature burns”: about 44°C (111°F) for 3–4 hours, 46°C (115°F) for 30–60 minutes, and 50°C (122°F) for 2–3 minutes (NITE). In a study of 206 burn-center patients in Beijing, 43.7% of low-temperature burns came from devices used to keep warm, 98.5% were third-degree and 70.9% needed surgery (Xie and colleagues, International Wound Journal, 2023). A pediatric case report describes a 9-year-old with a second-degree burn from a disposable toe warmer on bare skin, with no pain for about two hours (Contemporary Pediatrics, Dec 9, 2019).

Line that up with the picks: the THAW 2K tops out at 113°F, the Zippo at 120°F, the original THAW 5K at 140°F, HotHands packets average about 135°F, and Outdoor Life measured 140°F on the Unihand. That is why our pick is the gentlest one, and why the rule for all of them is the same: a glove or a pocket lining between the warmer and your skin, and move it around.

How do you use and charge a rechargeable warmer safely?

Charge it while you are awake and nearby with the cable it came with, keep a layer between it and your skin, and retire it the day it swells, smells or gets hot while off.

The six steps from the three 2026 recall notices and the makers’ recall pages. The step people skip is the first one: reading the bottom before the first charge.
  1. Match the model number to the list above before the season’s first charge.
  2. Charge only while you are awake and nearby, never overnight or while you are out, and only with the charger that came with it or one the maker recommends. That is CPSC’s general lithium-ion battery advice (CPSC); Zippo’s help page also says to use only the cable provided.
  3. Charge on a hard surface, not on a bed, a sofa or under a pillow, where heat cannot escape.
  4. Keep a layer between warmer and skin and do not use it while sleeping (makers’ manuals; NITE).
  5. Stop using a battery that is swollen, dented, leaking or smells, as Consumer Reports advises for lithium-ion packs.
  6. Dispose of it at household hazardous waste, never in the trash.

Quick answers

Which rechargeable hand warmers were recalled in 2026?

Three, per the CPSC: OCOOPA (about 1.5 million, July 30, No. 26-659), electric HotHands model H163650 (about 77,000, Oct 1, No. 27-011) and Gobi Heat model UT3053 (about 5,500, Oct 1, No. 27-009).

How do I tell if my OCOOPA hand warmer is recalled?

Read the model number on its underside. UT3053 (UT3 Lite), UT3056 (UT4 Young), ZLS-118, ZLS-118S, ZLS-118D, H01 and H01(PD) are recalled. Mark it “RECALLED”, photograph it with the batch number and submit it on ocoopa.com.

Are HotHands recalled?

Only the rechargeable electric HotHands, model H163650, white with orange and a white logo. HotHands says its air-activated warmers are not involved.

Are rechargeable hand warmers safe?

A warmer from a brand with no recall, charged while you are awake with its own cable and kept off bare skin, is a low risk. The OCOOPA recall shows what goes wrong otherwise: 1,480 overheating reports and 350 burn injuries.

Can rechargeable hand warmers catch fire or explode?

Yes. The OCOOPA notice says the lithium-ion battery can overheat and ignite, and lists 15 fires. Stop using any warmer that swells, smells or gets hot while switched off.

Are rechargeable hand warmers safe for kids?

Not for infants or small children, per makers’ packaging, and only with an adult checking for older kids. Children may not notice a slow burn building; a pediatric case describes no pain for about two hours.

Can you take a rechargeable hand warmer on a plane?

In carry-on, yes, if the battery is under 100 watt-hours (FAA); the TSA allows hand warmers. A recalled warmer may not be carried at all.

Rechargeable vs disposable hand warmers: which is cheaper?

Disposables, unless you use them often. At $0.75 a pair (Target, Nov 25, 2026), the $24.99 THAW 2K pair pays back after about 33 days.

How long do rechargeable hand warmers last?

Per charge, less than the box says: about 5 hours for a Zippo 9s rated at 9 in Bob Vila’s test, 1.5 to 3 hours for a THAW 2K rated at 4 in Target reviews. Zippo rates the 9s at 1,800 hours of life.

How do I dispose of a recalled hand warmer?

Mark it and send the refund photo first, then take it to a household hazardous waste site after calling ahead. Not the trash, curbside recycling or a store battery bin, the OCOOPA notice says.
