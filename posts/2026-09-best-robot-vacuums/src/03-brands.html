Brand by brand: what each one is actually good at

US shoppers now choose among seven brands that matter and a long tail that doesn't. Here is each one's real positioning — where it wins, where it loses, what its owners complain about, and who should buy it.

Roborock — the premium all-rounder with the best app

Positioning: the safe premium choice. Roborock sells at every price from the $300 Q7 M5+ to the $2,600 Saros Z70, and its lineup is the most confusing in the category: Q-series (Q5 Max+ vacuum-only, Q7 M5/M5+, Q10 S5+) are LiDAR budget robots with self-empty docks; Qrevo (S5V, Curv 2 Pro, Curv 2 Flow, Edge 3 Pro) are mid-to-upper robots with omni docks; Saros (10, 10R, 20, 20 Sonic, Z70) are the flagships, and the "R" denotes the turret-free StarSight navigation. Strengths: the best obstacle avoidance in the category (Saros 10R: 24/24), the most polished app, quiet operation, consistently strong lab results, and the slimmest flagship bodies. Weaknesses: the priciest of the big three at list; mopping with spinning pads is merely good until you step up to the roller on the Curv 2 Flow; the suction marketing has become absurd (36,000 Pa on the Saros 20 that measured below average). Owner complaints: few and scattered — the most consistent are the auto-empty port clogging in heavy pet-hair homes and the occasional map reset after firmware updates. Buy Roborock if you want the robot that will annoy you least over three years and you can wait for a sale; Roborock's list prices are aspirational and its sale prices are the real ones.

Saros 10 (retractable LiDAR turret, VibraRise mop) versus Saros 10R (turret-free StarSight, dual spinning pads): same body, different navigation and mop. The 10R is the one to buy on sale.

Dreame — the spec-sheet value king

Positioning: flagship features for noticeably less money. Dreame (also owner of the Mova brand) shipped the first mainstream climbing legs (ProLeap), the lowering LiDAR turret (VersaLift), and hot-water mop washing at prices Roborock charges $300–$500 more for. Lineup: D-series (D10 Plus Gen 2, ~$180–$280) budget LiDAR with a 90-day bag; L-series (L40 Ultra, L50 Ultra) upper-mid omni robots; X-series (X50 Ultra, X50 Master) flagships; Aqua10 Ultra Roller the new roller-mop flagship at 30,000 Pa. Strengths: the best threshold climbing in the business, excellent pet-hair results, huge value at the L50 Ultra's current price. Weaknesses: a younger US support operation; the Aqua10's roller drew mixed reviews (Gizmodo's verdict was "just buy a mop" while RTINGS and Vacuum Wars rated it highly); the app is good but a step behind Roborock's. Owner complaints: mop pads that need replacing sooner than expected, some units returning to dock mid-job on complex maps, dock water-tank alerts. Buy Dreame if you have thresholds or thick rugs, or you want 90% of the Roborock experience for 60% of the money.

Ecovacs — the feature experimenter, and the roller-mop pioneer

Positioning: the most gadgets per dollar, and the company that made roller mops mainstream. Lineup: T-series (T80 Omni at 18,000 Pa and the 2026 T80S/T90 Pro Omni) put the OZMO Roller in the $700–$900 tier; X-series (X9 Pro Omni at 16,600 Pa, X9S Pro Omni at 30,000 Pa, X11 and X12 OmniCyclone with the bagless dock). Strengths: the OZMO Roller mops as well as anything on sale; the X11 OmniCyclone's bagless station and fast charging are genuinely useful; Vacuum Wars named the X9 Pro Omni its best overall robot of mid-2025 and the T80 Omni its #2 value. Weaknesses: software that is hit-or-miss — map editing, no-go zone reliability and firmware updates are the most common complaints among the big brands — and the most-cited customer support frustration of any major brand. Battery life on the X9 Pro Omni also fell short of claims in reviews. Owner complaints: support tickets that stall, the app losing maps, roller wear. Buy Ecovacs if mopping is your priority and you'll buy through Amazon (whose return window is your safety net); skip it if you have zero patience for app quirks.

eufy (Anker) — the quiet one, with the widest range

Positioning: Anker's home brand covers both ends of the market: the $150–$200 11S MAX, which has been an Amazon best seller for seven years, and the $1,600 Omni S2, which Vacuum Wars scored a flawless 24/24 on obstacle avoidance. In between, the Omni E28 (20,000 Pa, with a detachable handheld wet-cleaner in the dock) has been a sale-price standout at $630–$800. Strengths: the Omni S2 is the quietest flagship, its corner cleaning is the clearest generational improvement anyone shipped this year, and its DuoSpiral split brush is the best long-hair design in the category; eufy's support (through Anker) is consistently rated above the Chinese-brand average. Weaknesses: the S2's mopping is good but loses to the Narwal Flow 2 and the roller-mop Ecovacs; the S2's list price is steep, and the S1 it replaced had a well-documented carpet weakness. Owner complaints: the E28's roller mop leaving streaks on dark floors; 11S MAX units getting stuck under low furniture (it's 2.85 in tall and goes places it can't get out of). Buy eufy if noise matters, you have long-haired people or pets, or you want a $150 robot that simply works.

Narwal — the mopping specialist

Positioning: Narwal built its name on mopping (its early Freo robots were mop-first) and the Flow 2 is the payoff: a FlowWash roller washed at 212°F in the dock, a dust bag rated for 120 days, and a dual-camera "visual language model" that judges how dirty a floor is and re-cleans accordingly. Vacuum Wars' three-month test found strong mopping, strong pet-hair handling and strong obstacle avoidance. Weaknesses: the app is the most criticized among the flagship brands, several of the AI features are more demo than daily-use, and it's a smaller company with a thinner US service footprint. List price is $1,499 with a $1,099 launch promotion that has recurred. Owner complaints: app map editing, occasional dock alignment failures, roller drying time. Buy Narwal if visibly clean hard floors are the whole point and you'll tolerate an app that is a generation behind Roborock's.

Shark — carpet and bagless docks on a budget

Positioning: Shark owns the $300–$500 tier in the US and is the brand most Americans see in Costco, Target and Walmart. Its 2026 lineup is built around PowerDetect (DirtDetect, FloorDetect and EdgeDetect sensors that boost power where they sense dirt, carpet or an edge): the PowerDetect Self-Empty vacuum-only with a bagless 60-day HEPA bin ($330–$430 on sale), the PowerDetect NeverTouch combo that also refills water ($430), and the NeverTouch Pro that washes and dries its mop pad too. Strengths: the best carpet deep-cleaning score in its price range, bagless (no ongoing bag cost), a 2.7-inch body that fits under nearly anything, and a US-based support organization. Weaknesses: obstacle avoidance and app polish a full tier behind the Chinese brands; a small battery that needs recharge-and-resume in larger homes (Vacuum Wars called its battery scores among the worst in its price range); the NeverTouch Pro's pet-hair pickup and edge cleaning disappointed reviewers. Owner complaints: getting stuck on rug fringe and cords, the 60-day bin's dust puff when emptied, and the mop pad needing replacement often. Buy Shark if you have mostly carpet, a budget under $450, and no cables on the floor.

iRobot (Roomba) — the reboot

Positioning: the brand Americans know, in year one of a new life. The pre-bankruptcy Roomba Max 705 Vac ($899.99 list; usually far less) is a LiDAR robot with iRobot's dual rubber anti-tangle rollers, an AutoEmpty dock and no camera — reviewers at RTINGS, Consumer Reports and Tom's Guide found strong pickup, particularly on hard floors and with long hair, and a simple, reliable app. The Max 705 Combo adds a mop with an AutoWash dock and trades a little carpet suction for it. The Roomba 105 Combo is the budget entry and sells in volume on Amazon. In May 2026 the new owners announced eight redesigned models (115 through 775); at the time of writing most are just reaching US retail, and we have no long-term owner data on them yet. Strengths: the rubber rollers really are the best long-hair design; the app is simple; iRobot robots remain the least likely to be confused by black rugs or dark floors. Weaknesses: no camera-based obstacle avoidance on the 705 (it will not dodge the charging cable or the pet accident), docks that only empty (no mop washing on the vac model), and a company whose support and firmware roadmap are in transition. Owner complaints: mixed reports on support response since the bankruptcy, older j/i/s-series apps losing features. Buy iRobot if you have long hair and mostly hard floors, you want no camera in your home, and you're buying at a deep discount through Amazon so its return window backs you up.

The budget tier: Tikom, Lefant, and the rest

Amazon's sub-$200 shelf is dominated by robots from brands that did not exist five years ago. Some are decent for the money; most share the same failure pattern in reviews: excellent first month, then charging failures, lost docks, spinning in place, and a support address that doesn't reply. Our rule is simple. Under $200, buy the eufy 11S MAX — Anker has made it for seven years, replacement parts exist, and it does exactly one thing (daily hard-floor and low-pile sweeping) reliably. Between $200 and $350, buy a LiDAR robot from a major brand: Roborock Q7 M5+, Dreame D10 Plus Gen 2, or Shark PowerDetect. The Mova V50 is the honorable mention for pet owners a tier up at $600–$900.
