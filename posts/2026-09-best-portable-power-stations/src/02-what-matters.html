The ten things that actually matter

A power station has two numbers on the front of the box and about ten that decide whether you'll be happy with it. The two on the box — watt-hours and watts — are the fuel tank and the engine. Everything else is what the reviews are really about.

1. Capacity (Wh) is the fuel tank — and the number everyone gets wrong

Watt-hours tell you how much energy is stored: a 1,024 Wh station holds enough to run a 100-watt load for about ten hours before losses. Two corrections apply to every unit. First, the inverter that turns battery DC into wall AC wastes 10–15% (OutdoorGearLab measures usable AC capacity between 80% and 90% of the label); plan on 85%. Second, real appliances don't draw their nameplate rating continuously. A refrigerator rated at 700 W surges to that for a second when the compressor starts, then averages 100–150 W across an hour of cycling; a "1,000 W" microwave draws 1,400 W from the wall but only for four minutes. The five-minute sizing method below handles both. The classes divide as follows: about 300 Wh for phones, laptops, camp lights and a single CPAP night; about 1,000 Wh for a fridge plus phones and lights through a night; about 2,000 Wh for the essentials of a household for a day, and multi-day with solar; 4,000 Wh and up for whole-home essentials, 240 V appliances and expansion.

2. Output (W) is the engine — check the surge, not just the continuous rating

The continuous rating is the most the inverter can supply all day; the surge (or peak) rating is what it can supply for a few seconds while a motor starts. Motors and heating elements are where stations trip: fridge and freezer compressors, sump and well pumps, window air conditioners, coffee makers (900–1,400 W), microwaves (1,200–1,500 W), hair dryers and space heaters (1,500 W). A 1,500 W continuous / 3,000 W surge unit like the Jackery 1000 v2 starts a 1/3 HP sump pump; the 2,000 W / 3,000 W C1000 Gen 2 does it with margin and can run a fridge and a microwave at once; the 600 W Elite 30 V2 runs a fridge but not the coffee maker. The "power lifting" or X-Boost modes on many units let a 1,800 W inverter run a 2,200 W kettle by dropping the voltage — fine for resistive heat, useless for motors and electronics, and the reason a "2,200 W" claim in a listing needs reading twice.

3. Chemistry: LiFePO4 or nothing

Every pick in this guide uses lithium iron phosphate (LFP, LiFePO4) cells, rated for 3,000–6,000 charge cycles to 80% capacity against 500–800 for the NMC cells in older and cheaper units. LFP is also far more stable thermally — the EcoFlow recall involved an older NMC-era design — and tolerates sitting at full charge better. It costs weight: about 25 pounds for 1 kWh, 40–55 for 2 kWh. If a listing doesn't say LiFePO4 in the title, assume it isn't.

4. Recharge speed and inputs: how you'll refill it decides how useful it is

Four ways in: the wall (the 2026 units fill a 1 kWh station in 49–70 minutes in their fast mode, two to three hours in quiet mode), solar (through an MPPT input rated in watts and a voltage window — the number to match to a panel), a car's 12 V socket (100–200 W, so eight to ten hours for 1 kWh; useful on a road trip, marginal in an outage) and, on the big units, a gasoline generator or a second station. Fast AC charging is the feature that changes outage strategy: when the grid returns for an hour at 2 a.m., you bank a full charge instead of a fifth of one. Every headline charge time also needs the fast mode switched on in the app and a circuit that can supply 15–20 amps — a common owner complaint is "slower than advertised" from a unit still in its default mode.

5. UPS switchover: under 10 ms is the new normal

Plug the station into the wall and your devices into the station, and a UPS (or "EPS") mode passes grid power through until the grid fails, then switches to battery. The switchover time is what matters: under 10 ms — now offered by the DELTA 3 Plus, the C1000 Gen 2 and Bluetti's Elite series down to the $219 Elite 30 V2 — keeps a desktop, a router, a CPAP, a modem, an aquarium pump or a garage-door opener running without a flicker; 20 ms (Jackery's UL-certified figure on the HomePower 3000) is fine for nearly everything; the 30 ms-plus of older designs can reboot a PC. Two caveats owners learn the hard way: the pass-through load is limited by the wall outlet, and many units count time in UPS mode against their fan and idle-drain budgets, so set the app's charge ceiling to 80–85% to protect the cells and expect the fans to spin up when the battery takes over.

6. Ports: count the ones you'll use, and look for the DC ones

Beyond the number of AC outlets (three or four on a 1 kWh unit; note whether any is a 20 A socket), the ports that matter are USB-C PD at 100–140 W, which runs a laptop straight from the battery without waking the inverter and its 10–15% loss; a 12 V car socket, which lets you run a CPAP or a 12 V camping fridge on a DC cord for 30–40% more runtime than through AC; and on home-backup units a TT-30 RV outlet, a NEMA 14-50 or L14-30 for 240 V and, on the F3800, EV charging. The C300 DC is the extreme case — no AC inverter at all, two 140 W USB-C ports — and it's the right answer for a surprising number of buyers who never plug in anything with a three-prong plug.

7. Solar: real panels deliver 60–75% of the number on the box

A "200 W" folding panel makes 120–150 W in good midday sun, and 0.8–1.2 kWh across a clear day; a 400 W panel array makes 1.5–2 kWh. That's enough to keep a 1–2 kWh station and a refrigerator going indefinitely in sunny weather and to extend a 4 kWh unit through a week. The numbers to match are the station's maximum solar input (100 W on the C300 DC; 200 W on the Elite 30 V2; 400 W on the Jackery 2000 v2; 600 W on the C1000 Gen 2; 1,000 W on the DELTA 3 Plus, DELTA 3 Max, Elite 100 V2 and Elite 200 V2; 2,600 W on the DELTA Pro 3; 2,400–3,200 W on the F3800 and F3800 Plus) and its voltage window, which must include the panel's open-circuit voltage. Third-party rigid panels are cheaper per watt than brand-name folders and connect through an MC4-to-XT60 or MC4-to-DC8020 adapter; the brand's own panel is the one that will work on day one.

8. Expandability and 240 V: buy the platform, not just the box

The DELTA 3 Plus grows to 5 kWh with expansion batteries, the DELTA 3 Max Plus to 10 kWh (the plain DELTA 3 Max does not expand), the DELTA Pro 3 to 12 kWh on one unit and 48 kWh across three with the Smart Home Panel 2, and the F3800 to 26.9 kWh with six expansion packs; Jackery's 2000 v2 and Bluetti's Elite 200 V2 do not expand at all, which is fine if you'll never need more. Only the 4 kWh class delivers 240 V split-phase — the DELTA Pro 3 and the F3800 do it from a single unit, Pecron's E3800LFP needs two — and 240 V is what a well pump, an electric dryer, a central-air air handler or a Level 2 EV charger needs. If any of those is on your list, the choice is between those two units and nothing else in this guide.

9. Weight, handles, wheels — and where it lives

The most-repeated three-star review in the category is a variant of "heavier than the photos suggest." A 1 kWh unit is 24–28 lb, a comfortable one-hand carry to the car; 2 kWh is 40–55 lb, a two-hand lift and the limit for most people to move alone; 4 kWh is 100–130 lb and lives on its wheels in a garage or closet, rolling to where it's needed. Decide where it will be stored (LFP prefers 50–80% charge and room temperature — not a hot attic, not a freezing shed) and how far it has to travel in the dark, then buy the class that fits.

10. App, idle drain, noise and the company behind it

Every big-brand station now has an app for firmware, charge limits, charging speed, port switching and remote monitoring; EcoFlow's is the deepest and the only one that insists on an account before it will update firmware, which some owners resent. Idle drain — what the unit eats just being switched on — ranges from 9 W on Anker's C-series (Anker says 37.5% below the industry average) to several times that on older and larger units, which is why big stations lose "a few percent a day" in owner reports; turn the AC side off when nothing needs it. Fan noise is the number-one complaint across all brands: fast charging and loads above 50% run the fans hard, quiet charging modes exist on every one, and the DELTA Pro 3's quiet mode is genuinely living-room quiet. Finally the company: warranties run 5 years on the current EcoFlow, Anker and Jackery lines and 5–6 on Bluetti's, but owner reports of support turnaround are not equal — Anker's is consistently praised, Bluetti's consistently not — and in a product you'll own for a decade that is a spec.

Sizing in five minutes: the method, and a chart to skip it

Write down what has to stay on, its average draw in watts and the hours it must run. Multiply, add them up, divide by 0.85 for the inverter, and add 20% for cold weather, an ageing battery and the things you forgot. That's your watt-hours. Then check the largest single load and the largest motor against the continuous and surge ratings. Three worked examples cover most readers:

  • A CPAP through the night. 30–40 W without the heated humidifier, 8 hours: 320 Wh ÷ 0.85 = 375 Wh. A 288 Wh unit makes it if you run the machine on its 12 V DC cord (skipping the inverter, so 288 Wh is nearly 288 usable) and turn the humidifier off; a 1 kWh unit gives two to three nights, or one with the humidifier on, which can triple the draw. Medical equipment is the one case where we say buy the next class up and the solar panel.
  • The fridge, the router and the phones for a night. Fridge 120 W average for 12 hours (1,440 Wh), router and modem 15 W (180 Wh), four phones and a laptop (100 Wh): 1,720 Wh ÷ 0.85 = about 2,000 Wh with margin. A 1 kWh unit does it if you run the fridge in cycles (it holds temperature for four hours closed); a 2 kWh unit does it without thinking.
  • The 72-hour outage. The same essentials for three days is roughly 4 kWh a day — 12 kWh in total, which nobody carries. The realistic plan is a 2 kWh station plus a 400 W panel (1.5–2 kWh a day in sun), or a 4 kWh station plus the panel, plus discipline: keep the fridge closed, run it 20 minutes an hour in the cool of the night, charge devices from USB-C, and turn the inverter off between jobs. That plan works; a bigger battery alone does not.
