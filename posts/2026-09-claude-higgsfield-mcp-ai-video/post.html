How does the Claude + Higgsfield workflow actually work?

Claude is the director and never touches a credit. Higgsfield is the studio and charges per take. The three-step rule keeps the studio from running the meter while the director is still thinking.

The routine every tutorial converges on. The plan step is free, the stills are cheap, the video is where the money goes, so nothing reaches step 3 that was not approved in step 2.

Higgsfield's MCP server, launched at the end of April 2026, is a hosted connection that lets an assistant call Higgsfield's tools from inside a conversation: generate an image, generate a video from text or from an image, train a reusable character, check a job's status, list your characters. Higgsfield describes it as opening 30-plus models through one door, and the model list on the MCP page reads like the front page of the AI-video industry: Kling 3.0, Veo 3.1, Seedance 2.0, Wan 2.6, MiniMax Hailuo, plus Higgsfield's own Soul photo model and Cinema Studio. Images come back at up to 4K, video at up to 15 seconds, in 16:9, 9:16, 1:1 or 4:5. The connector works in Claude on the web, in the desktop app, in Cowork and in Claude Code, and Higgsfield publishes the same address for ChatGPT and Cursor.

What the connector does not do is think. That is the part the Oppadu lesson spends most of its time on, and it is why the channel wrote five skills instead of a single "make video" command. A skill, in Claude's vocabulary, is a saved procedure the assistant follows when you type its name. The five from the lesson:

  • higgs-ads-scripter: give it a product photo or a shop link and it writes an ad plan as an HTML file: hook, scenes, lines for long-form and for Shorts, and a check of the copy against Korea's Fair Labeling and Advertising Act. (A US version of that check is the twelve-line list at the end of this page.)
  • higgs-ad-creator: turns the approved plan into the poster and the short-form clip.
  • higgs-3d-shorts-creator: a character plus a topic becomes a viral-style Shorts plan, image drafts, then video.
  • higgs-explainer-studio: a topic becomes a 3D-animation-style explainer with narration audio.
  • higgs_logo_studio: a new logo, or a clean-up of the old one.

Every one of those skills does its planning without spending a credit; the credits go only when an image or a video is actually requested. Higgsfield's own skills repository, published for Claude Code and similar agents, covers the same ground from the vendor's side with nine procedures: a general generator, a Soul ID character trainer, a product photoshoot, a brand kit, marketplace listing cards, website visuals, a video explainer, YouTube thumbnails and game assets. One command installs the lot. Between the two sets you get the thirteen jobs in the next section.

The same 15-second ad started two ways. The Monday version is the one every tutorial warns about; the Wednesday version is the one the plan step exists for.

Two warnings run through every serious tutorial, Korean and American alike. First, anything generated through the MCP or the command line always deducts credits, even on plans Higgsfield sells as unlimited; the unlimited part applies on the website only. Second, Claude's connector settings let you decide, tool by tool, whether the assistant may act on its own. The Oppadu lesson has you block the billing tool outright and set the four generating tools to "approval required", so that a misread sentence cannot turn into forty renders. Do that before anything else.

How do you set it up?

Six steps, about ten minutes, no code unless you choose the Claude Code route. Step 4, the permissions, is the one people skip and regret.

The order that works. Steps 1–3 are Higgsfield's own instructions; step 4 is the Oppadu lesson's addition; step 6 is the habit that keeps the credit balance intact.
  1. Open Claude's connector settings. In the web or desktop app: Settings → Connectors → Add custom connector. Custom connectors are a paid-plan feature, so this is where Claude Pro becomes necessary.
  2. Paste Higgsfield's MCP address. Name the connector "Higgsfield" and paste the address from Higgsfield's MCP page (it is on the mcp.higgsfield.ai domain). There is no API key to find; that is the point of the design.
  3. Connect and sign in. Click Connect, log in with your Higgsfield account in the browser window that opens, and return. The tools now appear in the chat's tool list.
  4. Set the permissions. Click the Higgsfield entry in the connector list. Block the billing tool (the lesson names it "Cancel Auto-Renewal"). Set the image, video, character and voice tools to "approval required". If everything is left on "always allow", the assistant can generate without asking, and a vague sentence becomes a bill.
  5. Add the skills. Either Oppadu's five files from the lesson page (Korean prompts, English-friendly outputs), or Higgsfield's official set from its GitHub repository with the command in the box below. Skills are text; read them before you run them.
  6. First run: plan only. Upload one product photo. Ask for a 15-second plan with three shots, an aspect ratio and a voice line. Read it. Fix it. Generate nothing until the shot list is right. That run costs zero credits, and it is the run that teaches you what the model needs to hear.
# Claude Code, one line (browser sign-in on first use):
claude mcp add --transport http --scope user higgsfield https://mcp.higgsfield.ai/mcp

# Higgsfield's official skills for Claude Code and similar agents:
npx skills add higgsfield-ai/skills

# Higgsfield's own CLI, if you prefer the terminal to the chat:
npm install -g @higgsfield/cli && higgsfield auth login

Three things go wrong often enough to plan for. A pasted address that will not connect (the error shows up in Anthropic's public issue tracker; disconnecting and reconnecting, or re-pasting the address without a trailing space, fixes most cases). An expired login, which looks like a dead connector and is fixed the same way. And queue timeouts on free or trial Higgsfield accounts, which get lower priority than paid ones; if a job "fails" but the credits are gone, check the history page on the website before retrying, because the render may have finished after the chat gave up.

How do creators actually get paid for this?

Four ways, and every one of them has a gate: a client's sign-off, a listing you already sell, a platform's threshold, or a reader who subscribes through a disclosed link. Results vary; most people who try earn little or nothing, and the numbers in video titles are marketing.

The four routes the Korean and American creators describe, with what each requires before it pays. Income figures are left off on purpose: the ones in circulation are vendor claims.

A client pays per video. This is the model the Oppadu skills are built for: a product photo or a shop link in, an ad plan out, a poster and a clip after approval. The Korean marketing agencies that adopted it sell product detail pages and 15-second ads to local shops, clinics and restaurants. One creator published his whole pipeline as a script: keyframes, five-second Kling clips, a crossfade, subtitles, music and narration, from one headshot to an 18-second promo, for 25 credits of video. The gate is the client's written approval of the plan before any credits are spent, and a generation log you can hand over with the file.

Your own listing sells more. Amazon, Etsy and Shopify sellers use jobs 4 and 5 (the photoshoot and the marketplace cards) and job 3 (the short ad) for products they already sell. The rule is the product as it is: no invented features, no sizes it does not come in, and no AI "customer" saying how much she loves it, because the FTC's fake-review rule treats an AI testimonial as a fake testimonial. Amazon's own video rules apply to a synthetic clip exactly as they apply to a filmed one.

A platform pays for views. YouTube's Partner Program needs 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 public watch hours in a year, or 10 million Shorts views in 90 days, before ad revenue starts, and YouTube has announced higher bars for new applicants from February 2027. Then the July 2025 policy decides whether AI clips qualify: "inauthentic content", mass-produced or templated videos with no real creator input, cannot be monetised, and in January 2026 YouTube terminated sixteen channels with a combined 35 million subscribers under it. YouTube says AI tools themselves are welcome when the final video adds unique human value; the losers were bot farms and near-duplicates. Shorts pay in the range of a few cents per thousand views, according to every 2026 creator survey we could find, which is why the faceless-channel arithmetic only works at scale, and why scale is what the policy punishes. Higgsfield's own creator campaigns (a weekly pool funded by brands, with reported per-video caps) are a separate pot, paid for reach, and a paid promotion under both YouTube's and the FTC's rules.

What rules keep an AI product video legal in the United States?

Twelve lines. They come from the FTC's endorsement and fake-review rules, the platforms' labels, state likeness laws and the tools' own licences, and they are the difference between an ad and a complaint.

The checklist we run on our own pages, in the order the problems usually arrive. It is a summary of the rules as they stood in September 2026, not legal advice.
  1. Disclose paid links before the click. The FTC's 2023 Endorsement Guides want disclosures that are unavoidable and in plain words; the FTC's own FAQ says "paid link" next to the link is adequate and "affiliate link" alone may not be. A footer does not count. This page marks its paid links and says so at the top.
  2. No fake or AI-written reviews. The Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule, in force since October 21, 2024, bans reviews and testimonials by AI or by people who never used the product, with civil penalties per violation reported above $50,000. An AI avatar praising a product is a fake testimonial no matter how it is labelled.
  3. No earnings promises. "Creators report" with a source and a date; never "you will make". If you ever quote an atypical result, the rule requires saying what people generally get.
  4. Label synthetic media where the platform asks. YouTube has required the altered-or-synthetic disclosure for realistic AI content since March 2024 and now applies labels itself; TikTok and Meta have their own. A photoreal AI clip of a "person" is the textbook case.
  5. Never generate a real person's face or voice without written consent. Tennessee's ELVIS Act (July 2024) covers voice as well as likeness, other states have similar rights of publicity, and the federal TAKE IT DOWN Act (May 2025) criminalises intimate deepfakes. Invented characters and consenting owners only; a dog is ideal.
  6. Commercial rights come with the paid tier. Free tiers of Kling, HeyGen and ElevenLabs are watermarked, non-commercial or both; Higgsfield's terms let you use outputs commercially and claim no ownership of them. Keep the invoice that proves which plan made the file.
  7. Own the music. Synthesise it, license it, or use a tool's licensed library. YouTube's audio library is licensed for YouTube videos, not for a clip hosted on your own site or a client's.
  8. Say what is AI in the ad itself. New York's synthetic-performer law took effect June 9, 2026 and requires a conspicuous disclosure when an ad uses a synthetic human performer, with fines from $1,000; California's transparency law (operative August 2026) binds the tool makers, not you, but the watermark it requires must not be stripped.
  9. Show the product as it is. The FTC's truth-in-advertising standard applies to synthetic footage. No invented features, colours, sizes or results.
  10. Keep the generation log. Prompts, dates, model names, plan level, credit receipts. It is your answer to a client, a platform and, if it ever comes to that, a regulator.
  11. Embed, never re-upload, other creators' videos. YouTube's terms license the embeddable player; they forbid downloading. A thumbnail is a separate copyrighted image, which is why the tiles on this page are text.
  12. Name tools, don't borrow their logos. Saying "Claude" or "Higgsfield" to describe what you used is nominative use and fine; using their logos, or implying they endorse you, is not. Anthropic's trademark guidelines and Higgsfield's terms both say so in as many words.

Two more that apply to a page like this one rather than to an ad: Google's reviews guidance wants first-hand evidence (our own plan, our own animatic, our own costs) and links to more than one place to buy; and Amazon's Associates agreement, updated April 14, 2026, wants its identification sentence on the page and no price claims that did not come from its own systems, which is why the desk-kit buttons below say "see today's price" rather than a number.

Quick answers

What is the Higgsfield MCP?

A hosted connector, launched in April 2026, that lets an AI assistant call Higgsfield's image, video and character tools from inside a chat. In Claude it is added as a custom connector by pasting the address from Higgsfield's MCP page and signing in; there is no API key. It exposes generation, status and character tools and opens the 30-plus models on the platform, with images up to 4K and clips up to 15 seconds.

Do I need a paid plan to use it?

Two of them, in practice. Custom connectors are a paid-plan feature in Claude, so Claude Pro is the floor. On the Higgsfield side you can connect with a free account, but every generation through the MCP spends credits, so you need a plan or prepaid credits to make anything. Planning in Claude, with or without the connector, costs no credits at all.

Does it work in Claude Code?

Yes. One command adds the server for your user account, the browser handles the sign-in on first use, and the same tools appear in the terminal session. Higgsfield also publishes a CLI and a skills repository aimed at Claude Code and similar agents, which is the route the batch-processing tutorials use.

How many credits does a five-second clip cost?

It depends on the model and resolution, and the prices change. In 2026 logs and reviews: about 25 credits for a five-second Kling 3.0 clip from an image, about 22 for a Seedance 2.0 clip, about 29 for a four-second Veo 3.1 clip. At the prepaid rates seen in August 2026 a credit cost roughly five to six cents. Run one clip, read the cost in your history, then multiply.

Why do the tutorials insist on planning first?

Because planning is free and video is the expensive step. A one-line request sent straight to a video model produces a pretty clip with the wrong product, and a failed generation costs the same as a good one. The plan fixes length, aspect ratio, camera moves and the product's exact appearance before a credit is spent, and the stills round catches mistakes at a fifth of the price of a clip.

Can I use the videos commercially?

From Higgsfield, yes: its terms claim no ownership of your outputs and do not restrict commercial use, and paid plans download without a watermark. Check the specific model's terms too, and use paid tiers of any voice or avatar tool you add, because their free tiers are non-commercial. Keep the invoice that shows the plan that made the file.

Do I have to label the videos as AI-made?

On YouTube, realistic synthetic content must carry the altered-or-synthetic disclosure, and YouTube now adds labels itself. In New York, an ad with a synthetic human performer must disclose it since June 9, 2026. Everywhere, an AI clip that misrepresents a product breaks ordinary truth-in-advertising rules. The safe habit is a caption that names the tool and the plan on every AI-generated asset.

Can I put a real person in the ad?

Only with written consent, and never a cloned voice or face of someone who has not agreed. Tennessee's ELVIS Act, other states' publicity laws and the federal TAKE IT DOWN Act all reach synthetic likenesses. The creators in this guide use invented characters, products, and in our case a dog.

Is Higgsfield safe to pay for after the December 2025 bans?

The service works and the connector is the best in the category; the company's billing history is the reason to pay monthly, read the refund terms (seven days, unused credits only, a fee, nothing on renewals), screenshot your plan page, and block the billing tool in Claude's connector settings so an assistant can never touch your subscription.

What is the cheapest way to try the whole workflow?

Claude's free tier for the plan, written in the format from the Noeul section; Higgsfield's smallest plan or a current trial for one round of stills and one clip; your own voice instead of a voice tool. That is about $9 to $30 for a first finished clip, and it tells you whether you want the $20-a-month connector at all.
